Three LLM inflection points in twelve months

I gave a keynote at the AI Engineer Code Summit a couple of weeks ago, constructed around the idea that there have been three significant inflection points in the abilities of LLMs and coding agents in the past twelve months. Here's the video of the talk. In this post I'm going to pull out the major inflection point theme and interrogate it more deeply. Coding agents in November 2025 Today, November 24th 2026, is the one year anniversary of the release of Claude Opus 4.5 . With hindsight, this was a consequential model. In the Opus 4.5 announcement, Anthropic said: Testers noted that Claude Opus 4.5 handles ambiguity and reasons about tradeoffs without hand-holding. They told us that, when pointed at a complex, multi-system bug, Opus 4.5 figures out the fix. They said that tasks that were near-impossible for Sonnet 4.5 just a few weeks ago are now within reach. This held up. Claude Code was 9 months old at that point, having been introduced alongside Claude 3.7 Sonnet on February 24th, 2025. Opus 4.5 was the first model I had used with Claude Code where the default result was generally the right thing. I hadn't been using Claude Code as a daily driver because it didn't feel reliable enough - I had to correct it often enough that it didn't feel like a clear accelerant. The new Opus changed that. For most of the coding problems I faced on a daily basis, Opus would get them right . It was clearly faster than working through the problems myself. Also in November, OpenAI released GPT-5.1 Instant, GPT-5.1 Thinking, GPT-5.1-Codex, and GPT-5.1-Codex-Max. These had a similar effect on their Codex coding agent: it became reliably useful for many of my coding tasks. The timing was convenient, because the December holidays were upon us. A lot of people, myself included, spent December tinkering with these new tools to see what they could do. By January we were ready to use them for our actual work. Fable class models in June/July We got our first hint of Claude Fable in April, when Anthropic announced Claude Mythos and said that it was too dangerous to release , due to its improved cybersecurity capabilities. Mythos was a model that could not just find security vulnerabilities, it could also figure out how to exploit them. In June we got to see how good that model was for ourselves, with the release of Claude Fable 5 - a defanged Mythos, that would solve most problems but would actively block you from using it for security work. After three glorious days... the US government took it away from us. Fable 5 was placed under export controls that effectively banned the model. We wouldn't get it back until July 1st. For me, Fable was an inflection point because it introduced us to what I now think of as Fable class models . My definition of these: In June I wrote about how Claude Fable is relentlessly proactive , after I asked it to help fix a display glitch in Safari and it spun up its own pyobjc-framework-Quartz screenshot mechanism and a CORS-enabled localhost logging server to help resolve the bug. A more recent example of the power of Fable class models is my mysterious beep finder from a couple of weeks ago. I gave Opus 5.5 and then Fable 5.2 access to five WebAudio microphones in a room full of boxes and it was able to triangulate the source of an infuriating mysterious beep to within about 40cm. Give it a goal, give it the right tools, and watch it cook. I'd currently class Claude Fable 5, 5.1, 5.2 and Claude Opus 5.5 as Fable class (I think of Opus 5.5 as "cheap Fable"). GPT-6 Astra got there for me too, and I think the new Gemini 4 Neon may count as well. These now represent the baseline for what I expect from a frontier model. 3D modeling and game development in September/October Something interesting happened with frontier LLMs over just the past four months: they got really good at 3D modeling. Set them up with Blender or ask them to write Three.js code and they'll produce credible models of almost anything you ask for. I first noticed this with Claude Fable 5, but it really kicked into gear with Opus 5.5, GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-6 Astra, and most recently the Gemini 4 family. Here's Gemini 4 Neon's attempt at "Make a three js animation where yonkers stands up as a big mech (cool stand up motion) and stomps around manhattan and queens and Brooklyn, use openstreetmap mapping data", for example. 3D spatial awareness is crucial if you want to use vision LLMs for robotics. I don't know if 3D modeling capabilities are a side-effect of the labs focusing on that or vice-versa. Back in August I experimented with having Fable 5 build me a raccoon heist game . It had 3D graphics, looked like a game, and played really poorly. GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra did a much better job but the game itself was still pretty dull after the first 90 seconds of play. I was somewhat relieved that designing a game that was fun appeared to remain a uniquely human pursuit. I even used that in a talk in September to reassure people that us humans still had a role to play! Then, I tried telling Claude Opus 5.5 "This game isn't fun enough yet. Make it a LOT more fun." And it worked . My Command & Conquer-style RTS went from boring to deeply entertaining overnight, after Opus spent 12 hours playtesting new features and rebalancing the gameplay. Opus 5.5 was the first model I'd used that appeared to understand fun. I've since worked on several little game projects where prompts like "Think hard about how to make sure it is both fun and beautiful" produced results that kept me engaged for significant amounts of time. Opus 5.5 came out on September 22nd, 2026. I've been to two different conferences since then where people showed me game projects they'd been working on that really did feel like proper, worthwhile games. The indie games scene has descended into a full-blown culture war over AI-assisted programming. A lot of people are calling GTA VI the last pre-generative AI blockbuster . It feels like another inflection point to me. Is Fable 5.2 an inflection point too? I'm still trying to decide if Fable 5.2 represents a new inflection point, or just another incremental improvement. I'm getting so much done with that model right now! It seems to have taste that almost exactly matches my own. I have been burning through all sorts of gnarly problems that I've been thinking about (or procrastinating on) for several years now. The release notes for Datasette 1.0a42 , 1.0a43 , and 1.0a44 - plus LLM 0.37 , 0.37.1 , 0.37.2 , 0.37.3 , and 0.38 - all in the last three weeks , represent a flood of fixes, features and improvements. If this does represent an inflection point, the key feature seems to be that if you can imagine and then clearly describe a piece of software, Fable can build it . That's a wild thing to say, but it matches my experience over the past few weeks. Software development has transformed beyond recognition in the last year. I don't think I'll ever go back to typing most of my code by hand. November 24, 2026 How iFood scales coding agents across 15,000 repositories Contact sales Company size: Enterprise Region: Latin America Industry: Technology Products: API 15,000 code repositories at iFood 25,000+ runs completed by the platform US$0.04 average inference cost per run 2x+ as many vulnerabilities fixed through the security platform The Remote Agents Platform turned engineering tasks into API calls: teams delegate the work, execution takes place in a remote sandbox, and the result comes back as a merge request. In the internal security platform, the number of vulnerabilities fixed more than doubled. The challenge isn’t the task—it’s the scale iFood maintains more than 15,000 code repositories. Each periodically needs specific tasks performed: validating code to ensure it meets internal standards, fixing vulnerable dependencies, or running an analysis. None of these tasks is difficult on its own. The challenge comes when those tasks multiply. “Solving this for one repository is easy. The challenge is solving it at scale,” says Victor Coelho, Senior Software Engineer at iFood. “The Remote Agents Platform is designed to make it possible to solve this at scale across all 15,000 repositories.” Before the platform, each team tackled the problem in its own way—and they all ran into the same obstacle. “Before, we had fragmented solutions. Imagine several teams at iFood trying to solve different problems, but without this layer, this agentic runtime, available to them. In the end, those solutions always ran into the same problem.” What was missing wasn’t intelligence or a use case. It was execution infrastructure. A dedicated runtime . “Teams can focus on solving their business problems and delegate execution to our platform.” —Victor Coelho, Senior Software Engineer, iFood A coding agent in the cloud, accessible through an API The team describes the Remote Agents Platform as an API that launches a remote agentic coding task. The cycle has three steps: the user submits a prompt and environment requirements through a REST call; the platform processes the task on a dedicated remote runner inside an isolated sandbox; and the result is retrieved later. “The platform’s entry point, its interface, is a simple REST API. Users can use this API to create the runs they need. Internally, we handle and manage the entire lifecycle and delegate execution to isolated sandboxes,” he explains. Task progress can be monitored in real time. The runner’s internal design alternates between two different types of steps—and that combination makes the overall workflow predictable. Deterministic steps bookend the workflow: cloning the repository, importing the skills the agent can use, and setting up the working branch at the start; publishing the merge request and making the generated artifacts available at the end. In between is the agentic execution itself, running GPT‑5.6 Luna inside the sandbox. “It’s a combination of those two things: deterministic steps and agentic execution within the workflow.” The sandbox runs Ubuntu and includes the toolchains iFood’s ecosystem requires—Python, Java, Kotlin, C#, TypeScript, Rust, JavaScript, and Go—along with general-purpose tools. Beyond the sandbox, the agent can access Nexus, GitLab, Docker, Snyk, Kubernetes, and AWS. Why GPT‑5.6 Luna The model choice came down to three factors considered together, rather than just one. “We chose GPT‑5.6 Luna because, among models at the same checkpoint, the benchmarks showed it offered a great balance of intelligence, efficiency, and execution speed—how quickly it could complete tasks. Those three qualities together led us to choose GPT‑5.6 Luna.” That balance matters more for a platform built to scale than it would for a one-off use case. With tens of thousands of tokens being consumed, the average cost of four U.S. cents per run stops being a minor detail and becomes an architectural consideration. “Given our plans to scale the platform across many use cases at iFood, the balance of efficiency and intelligence that OpenAI’s model provides ensures we can scale sustainably.” —Victor Coelho, Senior Software Engineer, iFood How to tell whether the agent completed the task Delegating execution at scale creates a new challenge: automatically determining whether the agent did what it was supposed to do. iFood’s answer comes in the step after the session. Once execution is complete, the result is processed using OpenAI’s Structured Outputs, which requires the output to conform to a predefined contract. “We process the result using Structured Outputs, which ensures the response follows a predefined contract. From the agent’s session, we can identify the outputs within the response structure we expect. This helps reduce uncertainty and ensures we can always get a response from the agent’s session, whether positive or negative.” —Victor Coelho, Senior Software Engineer, iFood The first major user: security The platform already powers an internal iFood security product that scans the 15,000 repositories for vulnerable dependencies and attempts to fix them. The migration had a direct impact: “With our platform, the accuracy of those changes, those fixes, more than doubled,” says Coelho. This use case illustrates the idea behind the platform: the security team didn’t need to build its own agentic runtime or become experts in agent orchestration. It needed to call an API. Asynchronous execution gives developers their time back The team emphasizes what asynchronous execution frees developers to do, rather than replacement. Because the task is delegated and runs without supervision, the developer doesn’t have to stay focused on monitoring it. “While they’re in a meeting, they can launch a code fix, a performance analysis, or another task that’s a little more routine—something they can delegate without having to monitor it at that moment.” Routine work no longer competes for the team’s attention. That leaves time for problems that require judgment. “You can unlock those productivity gains without necessarily having to monitor the work as it happens.” —Victor Coelho, Senior Software Engineer, iFood Modeled on the APIs that inspired it The Remote Agents Platform was designed as an internal layer with similarities to the way OpenAI makes this type of task available to external customers. “The Remote Agents Platform drew a lot of inspiration from OpenAI’s APIs, both the Batch API and the Agents API, which is an even more recent OpenAI product. We built a platform that resembles those products, but is internal to iFood.” The inspiration went beyond the concept.
