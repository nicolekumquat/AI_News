One of the features of using AI in financial services, explained Singh, is that it’s typically long-horizon work — although not long-running. In his AIE presentation, Singh d that agent tasks at Arca can take up to ten days. “It's very stop-start, there's many actors in between, many decision points, many handoffs,” he said. For this reason, Arca has developed an internal memory system . “So we have this persistence for every client ,” Singh said in our podcast discussion. “What has happened so far? What are we tracking for next? What needs to happen immediately in the next couple of days [and] what are we tracking for the long term? Which individual is responsible for which action? All of this is being tracked by our memory systems that our agents contribute to.” Arca uses models from frontier labs like Anthropic and OpenAI, with Claude its primary model provider. The company has built its own harness , which automatically selects the best model to use for a particular task, based on the company’s internal benchmarks. Owning the harness, rather than using a managed agent harness, also means that Arca has the flexibility to change model providers if it needs to. Safety is obviously key for financial services. Singh d that although agents do much of the work, an agent’s role is limited to analysis and proposing solutions. Trading and any other money movement is handled by a human advisor. “All of our safety restrictions are fully deterministic,” he said. “And they take the decision making off the agent's hands.” In other words, there are certain tools and tasks the agent can execute by itself — but not ones that involve risk to the client. How I Built the Most AI-Pilled Company in the World Don't be put off by the clickbait headline, this piece by Jacob Sansbury describing how they are building Spawn is a fascinating exploration of the bleeding edge of what's possible with agentic engineering against frontier models. Spawn is a massively multiplayer gaming environment where players can use prompts (to the in-game agent, Savi) to modify the game world. I fired it up on my phone for a few minutes and was able to prompt a castle into existence, then a dragon, then ride the dragon to fly around that castle. By Jacob's telling, Spawn is now being developed autonomously by a swarm of Fable-powered agents costing over $3 million in tokens per month ("we spend 30x payroll on AI tokens"). Our agents change the live code directly and ship to production hourly, so they read every error, bug report and metric themselves. About 60% of our work starts from a production error, a Savi bug report (Savi is the AI you build games with in Spawn), something odd in our logs, or a feature request. Savi files bugs herself. When a user hits one, or Savi hits one in the product, she files it. It goes straight to an agent that decides whether it's a real bug to fix, something we won't fix, or intentional design that needs a decision. Usually it becomes a change, delivered to that user within an hour, with a message in the same chat. This is continuous deployment turned up to 11: A loop this tight only works if it never stops, so we treat an hour without a ship as a SEV-0, our most severe kind of incident. Unshipped changes pile up, and every problem already in production lasts longer. The feedback slows down too. An agent can only keep a few hours of work in its head, so shipping faster than that actually helps it stay coherent. [...] We can roll back a broken change in 30 seconds, exactly where it's broken and nowhere else. When a monitor or a log alert fires, it starts or wakes an agent that owns the incident, and we break things on purpose to test that those recovery paths work. My eyebrows raised at least a dozen times reading this. I have trouble imagining this style of development working on software with higher stakes for when something goes wrong, but for a game this appears to be working surprisingly well. Jacob says that Fable 5 (and then 5.1) was the model that made this level of autonomy possible. Opus 5.5 doesn't quite hack it for them, and when they tried GPT-6 Astra to apply fixes from a security review everything stopped working: Every feature had been disabled and marked to be turned on again once the security fixes were done. Astra had gone through our whole product and made the insecure things secure by turning them off. These kinds of extreme experiments are why I'm excited about game development as a substrate for exploring the edges of what today's coding agents can do.
